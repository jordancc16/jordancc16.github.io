About

Growing up, numbers have always been the one thing that made sense to me. Since the beginning of my school days, math has felt natural, as if it were made for me. My journey with math has been a winding path of discovery, and though you might call it the process of elimination, it's clear it has always been the subject meant for me.

One of my favorite books is The Art of Problem Solving by Richard Rusczyk and Sandor Lehoczky. I love to spend hours analyzing math problems, examining every nook and cranny, exploring different paths to the same answer, and even breaking down those answers to unlock new perspectives and imagine what other questions could stem from the same concepts. It doesn't matter whether the question is the easiest one on the page, or if it is intricately complex with symbols I could barely recognize, I treat each one the same way, seeing them as an adventurous opportunity to learn something new.

I'm also fascinated by the concept of chaos theory. While researching chaos theory on my own, everything I thought I knew about math suddenly flew out the door. At first, chaos theory seemed contradictory. I couldn't understand how such simple and structured equations could eventually lead to the most random and unpredictable of results. However, I was determined to dive deeper into the peculiar subject and scrutinize every article and video I encountered online. Through long periods of intensive research, I came across a plethora of concepts, including the Butterfly Effect, the phenomenon where a minute change can develop and flourish into drastically different outcomes. These discoveries only dragged me further and further into my mission to grasp these idiosyncratic ideas. As I thoughtfully observed how rudimentary beginnings spiraled into complexity, I finally understood that what I once thought was pure chaos was, in reality, deterministic.

I began to see a reflection of myself within chaos theory. For the most part, I was originally drawn to math because of its simplicity. Whenever I was handed a worksheet, I was certain that each question would have a solution. Yet, over time, the math I worked with stopped being so straightforward, and I noticed that the paths to each answer lost its comforting sense of tidiness. This loss of comfort, however, allowed me to view complexity in a whole new light; I started to pay closer attention not only to numbers on a page, but to the small details in everything around me. For instance, whenever I play chess, my instinctive observations extend beyond the sixty-four squares of the board. Any subtle movements from my opponent can clue me in to their potential strategy, similar to a poker tell. I watch the way they shift their body weight, and I see their fingers hover over certain pieces, hinting at their ultimate intentions. As I've gotten more familiar with chaos theory, I've learned to not shy away from complexity, but to embrace it and work at it patiently, aware that even the most intricate systems emerge from the simplest of principles.

Clearance-Time Modeling for Emergency Building Sweeps

Our team's solution to 2025 HiMCM Problem A. We modeled a building as a weighted graph and derived a closed-form model for how long it takes responders to fully sweep it during an evacuation — splitting clearance time into travel, inspection, and verification, then testing it across open, grid, radial, and multi-floor layouts with a sensitivity analysis.

  • Sensitivity analysis

Modeling the Tides of Victoria Harbour

An Honors Precalculus project fitting trigonometric functions to 43 hours of real tide-height data from Hong Kong's Victoria Harbour. I built a sinusoidal model by hand and checked it against a Desmos regression, using R² to judge the fit and capture how amplitude and period drift across the day.

  • Trigonometric modeling
  • Regression

  • Data fitting
